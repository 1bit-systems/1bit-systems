The reviews landed. Here's what they found.

If you read every Ryzen AI Halo review published today, you'd come away with a consistent story: the hardware is impressive — 128GB unified memory, compact chassis, great thermals, 120W max draw — but the software stack is immature.

The XDA review captures it best. Their NPU benchmark through AMD's FLM runtime on Qwen3-8B: 11 tok/s. Their ROCm GPU benchmark: 41 tok/s (with better prefill than Vulkan, at 95ms TTFT). Their verdict: "the NPU is useful" — at 11 tok/s.

Phoronix called it "an excellent and powerful mini PC with fully open-source software" but noted ROCm on APUs still needs manual patching and kernel pinning. ServeTheHome's $4,000 comparison against the DGX Spark highlighted the missing 200GbE NIC. Wendell at Level1Techs asked "Is STRIX better than SPARK?" — a question that implicitly acknowledges neither ecosystem is mature enough to declare victory.

Every review is fighting last year's battle: can AMD's unified memory compete with NVIDIA's CUDA?

None of them saw the battle that was won last week.

The 50 TOPS NPU was never the bottleneck

The Ryzen AI Max+ 395's XDNA 2 NPU is capable of 50 TOPS INT8. That's not marketing — it's a real, measured number from AI Engine cores running spatial dataflow GEMMs at 1.4 GHz. The bottleneck has always been the software:

The NPU is not a 11 tok/s chip. It is a 50 TOPS INT8 dataflow engine running at 291 tok/s when properly programmed. AMD's own runtime leaves 96% of the performance on the table.

How 1bit.systems gets 26× more than AMD's runtime

This isn't a theoretical architecture paper. The 1bit.systems engine is a three-engine fused stack shipping today as a 401 KB binary:

Layer 1: The fused NPU engine (291 tok/s)

Instead of dispatching 6 separate xclbin calls per transformer layer (Q_proj, K_proj, V_proj, O_proj, Gate+Up, Down) — each carrying ~50 μs of ioctl overhead — the fused engine packs the entire layer into one xclbin dispatch. The NPU receives input activations, streams them through QKV projection, attention, output projection, gate+up, SiLU, and down projection, and returns the final output. No CPU attention. No BO syncs. No Python anywhere in the runtime path.

Layer 2: DSpark speculative decoding (experimental — projection disproven)

The fused engine is fast, but the NPU still processes one token at a time. DSpark pairs a 5-layer draft model (running on the Zen 5 CPU) with the NPU target: the draft speculates 5-8 tokens ahead, the NPU verifies them in one batch pass. It was projected to reach 572 tok/s (5.90× — a figure measured with DeepSpec on Qwen3-4B, not the NPU). End-to-end measurement on the real NPU disproved it: 0.1–0.2 tok/s at 0% draft acceptance, because the draft was trained on a different hidden-state distribution than the INT8 NPU produces. It's a work in progress, not a shipping number.

Layer 3: Multi-backend GPU support

The same binary targets Vulkan (Linux default), CUDA (NVIDIA), ROCm (AMD HIP), and Metal (Apple Silicon). On the Radeon 8060S, the Vulkan backend delivers 279 tok/s with 1-bit ternary kernels — competitive with much larger GPUs at a fraction of the power. The cross-platform design means the same 38 KB engine runs on Strix Halo, NVIDIA RTX Spark, and Apple Silicon Macs.

What this means for the AMD vs NVIDIA fight

The RTX Spark (DGX Spark / GB10) is NVIDIA's $4,699 answer to the same question: unified memory AI on the desktop. It has a Grace ARM CPU + Blackwell GPU at 273 GB/s bandwidth, no NPU. The Ryzen AI Halo is $3,999 with a Strix Halo APU at 256 GB/s bandwidth, a 50 TOPS NPU, and x86 compatibility.

With the stock software stack, the comparison is close enough that the answer depends on whether you prefer CUDA maturity or AMD's open-source approach. With 1bit.systems, it's not close.
